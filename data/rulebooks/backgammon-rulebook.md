# Backgammon Rulebook

## Table of Contents

*Backgammon is one of the oldest known board games, with roots stretching back nearly 5,000 years to the ancient Near East. Two players race their fifteen checkers around and off a board of twenty-four narrow triangles called points, their moves governed by the roll of two dice. It blends luck and skill: the dice decide what you may do, but strategy decides how to use them — and the doubling cube turns every game into a wager of nerve. This rulebook describes the standard rules as codified by the World Backgammon standards.*

## Equipment and Setup

The board is divided into four quadrants of six **points** each. Each player has a **home board** (or inner board) and an **outer board**, separated by a ridge down the center called the **bar**. The points are numbered for each player from 1 to 24 starting at that player's own one-point (in their home board); the numbering runs in opposite directions for the two players.

Each player has **fifteen checkers**, set up as a mirror image of the opponent's:

- Two checkers on your 24-point.
- Five checkers on your 13-point.
- Three checkers on your 8-point.
- Five checkers on your 6-point.

You also need **two dice**, a **dice cup** for each player, and a **doubling cube** — a die marked 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 used to track the stakes.

## Object of the Game

Move all fifteen of your checkers around the board into your own home board, then **bear them off** (remove them from the board). The first player to bear off all their checkers wins. Checkers always move in one direction — from higher-numbered points toward your one-point and off.

## Starting and Rolling

Each player rolls a single die; the higher number goes first and plays those two numbers (their own die and the opponent's) as the opening move. If the numbers are equal, both re-roll. Thereafter players alternate, each rolling two dice at the start of their turn.

## Moving the Checkers

The two dice show two separate moves. You may move one checker the total of both dice or two different checkers, one for each die. The number rolled is the number of points (pips) you move a single checker forward toward your home.

- A checker may be moved only to an **open point** — one that is not occupied by two or more of the opponent's checkers.
- The numbers on the two dice constitute **separate moves**. For example, with a 5 and a 3 you may move one checker 5 and another 3, or move one checker 8 pips — but only if the intermediate point (either the point 5 away or the point 3 away) is also open.
- **Doubles** are played twice: a roll of two of the same number lets you make **four** moves of that number.
- You must use **both** numbers of a roll if legally possible (all four for doubles). If only one number can be played, you must play that one; if either but not both can be played, you must play the **higher** one. If no legal move is possible, you forfeit your turn.

## Hitting and Entering from the Bar

A point occupied by a single opposing checker is a **blot**. If you move a checker onto a blot, the blot is **hit** and placed on the bar.

- A player with a checker on the bar must **enter** it into the opponent's home board before making any other move. You enter by rolling a number corresponding to an open point in the opponent's home board (a 1 enters on the opponent's one-point, etc.).
- If neither number allows entry (both those points are blocked by two or more enemy checkers), you forfeit your turn. If you can enter some but not all checkers, you enter what you can, then forfeit the rest of the roll.
- Once all your checkers are off the bar, any unused portion of the roll may be played normally.

## Bearing Off

Once you have moved **all fifteen** checkers into your home board, you may begin bearing off. You bear a checker off by rolling a number that corresponds to the point on which it sits (a 6 bears off a checker from your six-point, and so on).

- If there is no checker on the point indicated by a die, you must make a legal move using a checker on a higher-numbered point.
- If there are no checkers on higher points, you must (and may) bear a checker off the highest point that a die can reach.
- You are **not** obliged to bear off if you can make an otherwise legal move within your home board.
- If a checker is **hit** during the bear-off, it goes to the bar and you must bring it all the way around and back into your home board before bearing off any more.

## The Doubling Cube

Backgammon is usually played for stakes per point, and the **doubling cube** lets a player who feels ahead raise them. At the start of the game the cube sits in the middle, available to either player, showing 64 (meaning 1).

- Before rolling on your turn, you may propose to **double** the stakes. Your opponent must either **decline** — forfeiting the game and the current stake — or **accept**, taking the cube (now showing 2) onto their side.
- Only the player holding the cube may make the next double (a **redouble**), and there is no limit to the number of doubles in a game.
- **Beavers, Jacoby, and Crawford** are optional tournament rules layered on top of the cube; the core rule is simply propose-before-you-roll, accept-or-forfeit.

## Gammons and Backgammons

At the end, the win is worth the value shown on the doubling cube — but the margin of victory multiplies it:

- A normal win (opponent has borne off at least one checker) counts **single** (the cube value).
- If the loser has **not borne off any checkers**, it is a **gammon**, worth **double** the cube value.
- If the loser has not borne off any checkers **and** still has a checker on the bar or in the winner's home board, it is a **backgammon**, worth **triple** the cube value.

## Optional Rules

- **Automatic doubles:** if identical numbers are thrown on the opening roll, the stakes double once (the cube is turned to 2 but stays in the middle). Usually a limit of one is agreed per game.
- **Beavers:** when doubled, a player may immediately redouble (a "beaver") while retaining the cube.
- **The Jacoby rule:** gammons and backgammons count only as a single game unless the cube has already been turned — commonly used in money play to encourage doubling.
